CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
1.1	Introduction
The rapid evolution of digital financial technologies has fundamentally transformed how individuals and organizations manage, transfer, and oversee funds. Electronic wallet (e-wallet) systems have become integral to contemporary financial infrastructures, evolving from simple stored-value tools into multifunctional platforms supporting real-time transaction processing and personal financial management (Yang et al., 2021; Widjaja, 2025). These innovations contribute directly to global economic sustainability and align with Sustainable Development Goal 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), emphasizing the need for accessible, secure, and resilient financial systems that enhance productivity and promote equitable participation in the digital economy (United Nations, 2015; Rohaiza Kamis et al., 2024).
As digital ecosystems expand, user expectations for financial platforms have shifted from basic transactional capabilities to more advanced collaborative financial management. Modern users increasingly require systems capable of handling complex, multi-layered group obligations, scenarios that frequently involve multi-currency variances, tax-inclusive computations, and hybrid expense-allocation rules. However, the current financial technology landscape remains fragmented; existing e-wallet platforms generally treat transactions as isolated events, lacking the architectural flexibility to support these intricate collaborative workflows (Girdhar et al., 2024). This functional disconnect forces users to rely on disjointed external tools, highlighting the urgent need for a cohesive, algorithmically capable financial solution.
In parallel, software engineering research increasingly emphasizes the importance of distributed microservice-based architectures for modern financial systems to overcome the rigidity of legacy monolithic designs. This architectural paradigm allows critical components such as authentication, currency conversion, transaction processing, and notification services to operate independently, enabling modular scalability, improved resilience, and enhanced security (Smith & Lee, 2024). Such characteristics are essential for financial technologies requiring high availability, low latency, regulatory compliance, and the flexibility to integrate computationally intensive split-payment logic.
Therefore, this project proposes the design and implementation of a Secure Distributed Microservice-Based E-Wallet System featuring a React mobile frontend and a Spring Boot backend. The system bridges gaps between collaborative financial demands and scalable engineering requirements by integrating configurable hybrid split-payment logic, dynamic multi-currency handling, and structured collection management within a fault-tolerant microservice environment. Beyond functional capabilities, this project contributes an architectural reference model to support future research in developing secure, scalable, and computationally intelligent financial systems. 
1.2	Problem Statement
While the adoption of digital financial technologies has been widespread globally (Yang et al., 2021), the functional and architectural designs of many existing e-wallet systems remain inadequate for supporting complex, real-world collaborative financial management. Despite the convenience offered by current platforms, a functional disconnect exists between standard payment processing and the intricate requirements of group expense management (Marushchak et al., 2024). This section outlines the critical limitations in current e-wallets, the inadequacy of external auxiliary tools, and the architectural constraints that collectively motivate the need for a secure, distributed, and algorithmically flexible solution.
1.2.1	Problem Statement 1: Absence of Native Collaborative Expense Management in E-Wallets
The fundamental deficiency in the current e-wallet landscape is the lack of integrated, native support for collaborative finance. Most commercial e-wallet platforms are architected primarily for Peer-to-Peer (P2P) transfers or Business-to-Consumer (B2C) payments, treating transactions as singular, isolated events rather than interconnected group obligations (Yang et al., 2021). Consequently, users are unable to initiate, track, or settle shared expenses directly within the payment ecosystem. This functional void forces users to treat the "calculation" and the "payment" as two separate processes, creating a disjointed user journey where financial data is often lost or manually duplicated between disparate systems (Eskandarian et al., 2020).
1.2.2	Problem Statement 2: Inadequate Support for Customised and Hybrid Expense-Splitting Rules in External Tools
To mitigate the lack of native support in e-wallets, users currently resort to fragmented external tools—such as third-party splitting applications or manual spreadsheets—to handle group finances. However, these auxiliary solutions exhibit significant functional rigidity; they lack the high-level adaptability required to fully support real-world scenarios that involve tax-inclusive computations, mixed proportional and fixed-amount allocations, or hierarchical payment priorities (Marushchak et al., 2024).
Most existing tools are incapable of processing the "mixed version" of these constraints—specifically, the simultaneous convergence of variables where a single transaction requires applying tax calculations alongside hybrid division rules and secondary division hierarchies. Consequently, this lack of algorithmic depth forces users to rely on manual intervention, thereby increasing cognitive workload, operational inefficiency, and the probability of computational errors (Al-Khalifa et al., 2025)
1.2.3	Problem Statement 3: Poor Integration of Multi-Currency Handling in Group Transactions
A critical limitation affecting both native e-wallets and external splitting tools is the fragmented handling of multi-currency group transactions. Current systems often implement multi-currency functionality as an isolated feature for individual conversion rather than as an integrated component of shared financial management. This results in opaque conversion logic where exchange rates are not consistently applied across the group, making it difficult to track obligations when the payer uses one currency (e.g., USD) and the debtors settle in others (e.g., MYR or SGD) (Brunnermeier et al., 2019). This disconnection leads to "exchange rate drift," where the final settled amount differs from the recorded obligation, causing financial discrepancies and reducing trust in digital platforms for cross-border collaboration (European Central Bank, 2021).
1.2.4	Problem Statement 4: Scalability and Resilience Constraints of Monolithic System Designs
Beyond functional limitations, a significant underlying problem lies in the architectural design of legacy financial systems. Many existing platforms rely on monolithic architectures that, while suitable for simple direct payments, struggle to handle the high computational load required for dynamic bill splitting, real-time currency conversion, and concurrent notification dispatching (Megargel et al., 2020). In a monolithic structure, a failure in the "calculation module" can cascade to affect the "payment module," leading to total system unavailability. This architectural rigidity restricts the ability to scale individual components efficiently, underscoring the need for a distributed microservice-based architecture that ensures fault isolation and high availability for complex financial computations (Syed Ali Fathima, 2025).
1.2.5	Summary
Collectively, these limitations demonstrate that the current digital financial ecosystem is bifurcated: e-wallets handle payments but lack collaborative logic, while external tools offer basic splitting but lack algorithmic depth and integration. Addressing these gaps necessitates a unified, distributed, and algorithmically enriched e-wallet system capable of managing real-time multi-currency collaboration, advanced hybrid expense-splitting logic, and resilient financial processing.
1.3	Project Aim
To design and develop a secure, distributed, microservice-based e-wallet system using Spring Boot and React to provide flexible split-payment management and multi-currency financial operations, thereby enhancing the efficiency, transparency, and reliability of collaborative financial transactions.
1.4	Objectives
	To design and develop a secure, distributed microservice-based architecture using Spring Boot and React that ensures scalable, fault-tolerant, and consistent execution of financial transactions.
	To formulate and implement a flexible split-payment service capable of supporting complex expense-sharing scenarios, including tax-inclusive computations, dynamic allocation strategies (percentage, fixed, mixed), and hierarchical primary–secondary division rules.
	To construct a comprehensive multi-currency wallet module supporting MYR, SGD, and USD that facilitates accurate transaction recording, real-time currency conversion, and secure direct-deduction mechanisms for cross-currency payments.

1.5.2 Architectural & Technical Scope
To support the functional requirements, the system will be constructed using the following technical specifications.
	Architecture: The system will be deployed as a distributed microservice architecture, utilizing a database-per-service pattern to ensure data isolation, fault tolerance, and independent scalability of functional components (Richardson, 2018).
	Backend Technology: Java Spring Boot will be utilized to develop RESTful APIs and business logic services.
	Data Persistence: MySQL will be employed as the primary Relational Database Management System (RDBMS) to ensure ACID (Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability) compliance for all financial transactions and complex relationship mapping (Elmasri & Navathe, 2021).
	Frontend Technology: React will be employed to build a responsive, interactive mobile-web user interface.
1.5.3 Project Constraints
The development and execution of this project are subject to the following limitations.
	Simulated Banking Integration: Due to regulatory and security restrictions, the system will not integrate with live commercial banking APIs like FPS or SWIFT. All external bank deductions and transfers will be simulated using secure mock gateways to demonstrate functionality without actual financial risk.
	Currency Limitation: The prototype is strictly limited to three currencies (MYR, SGD, USD) to demonstrate multi-currency capability without the operational overhead of maintaining a global live exchange rate feed. 
	Target Scale: The system is optimized for small-to-medium collaborative groups (e.g., travel groups, household expenses) and is not designed to handle enterprise-level high-frequency trading or massive concurrent banking workloads.
1.5.4 Out of Scope
	Native Mobile Application Development: The client interface will be web-based (React); native iOS (Swift) or Android (Kotlin) mobile applications are not included.
	Advanced Financial Intelligence: The system will strictly provide historical reporting; it will not include predictive analytics, credit scoring models, or AI-driven fraud detection mechanisms.
	Complex Document Exporting: The reporting module is limited to on-screen digital views (Dashboards/tables). The generation of downloadable physical document formats (e.g., PDF, Excel, CSV) is explicitly excluded from this iteration.
	Blockchain & Cryptocurrency: The project focuses on relational database consistency and standard fiat currency management; it does not involve blockchain ledgers or cryptocurrency integration.
	Physical Hardware Integration: The project does not involve the integration of physical payment terminals (POS) or NFC card readers.
1.5.5 Summary
In conclusion, the defined scope establishes a clear roadmap for developing a Secure Distributed Microservice-Based E-Wallet System. The project prioritizes the technical complexity of the split-payment logic module, comprehensive financial reporting, and multi-currency consistency over broad commercial features. By leveraging a database-per-service microservice architecture, the system ensures data isolation, scalability, and ACID compliance. By strictly adhering to these functional boundaries and acknowledging the necessary operational constraints such as simulated banking and specific currency limits, the project ensures a rigorous, feasible, and academically significant implementation that directly addresses the research objectives.

All three context-specific roles (Admin, Editor, Member) share identical privileges regarding financial settlement. Regardless of hierarchy, every participant has the right and ability to view full transparency details (receipts and calculation logic), execute payments for their own share and authorize direct wallet deductions (including complex cross-currency settlements).
The Editor role is designed as a mirror of the Administrator for day-to-day operations but lacks "Destructive" or "Structural" control. The Member role represents the standard "consumer" of the system. Unlike Admins and Editors, Members have strictly Read-Only access to the "Input" side of the ledger (cannot add expenses or change logic) but fully functional access to the "Output" side (viewing debts and paying).
 
CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Domain Research
2.1.1 Overview of Financial Technology (FinTech) and E-Wallets
2.1.1.1 The Evolution of Digital Payments: From Cash to Mobile Ecosystems. The trajectory of global financial exchange has undergone a radical transformation, shifting from tangible, asset-backed mediums to abstract, decentralized digital ledgers. This evolution can be categorized into four distinct chronological and technological phases, each resolving the inefficiencies of its predecessor while introducing new layers of functional complexity.
Phase 1: The Era of Tangible Exchange (Cash and Barter). Historically, financial transactions were predicated on physical proximity and tangible assets. The barter system, while fundamental, suffered from the "double coincidence of wants," a limitation resolved by the introduction of standardized currency (Greenlaw & Shapiro, 2017). While cash provided immediate liquidity and anonymity, it imposed significant logistical costs including high security risks, physical transportation limits, and a lack of auditability. In the context of collaborative finance, cash remains the most inefficient medium because splitting bills requires precise denominations and physical presence, often leading to rounding errors and social friction.
Phase 2: The Card-Based Network (Plastic Money). The mid-20th century introduced the first major abstraction of money: the credit and debit card. This phase decoupled "payment" from "possession" and allowed users to transact without holding physical funds. This era was defined by a significant Technological Shift marked by the introduction of the ISO 8583 standard and magnetic stripes which allowed electronic verification via Point of Sale (POS) terminals. However, this system faced a critical Limitation; while it solved portability, card networks were heavily reliant on expensive banking infrastructure like ATMs and proprietary terminals. The high Merchant Discount Rates (MDR) associated with this model effectively excluded smaller vendors and informal peer-to-peer (P2P) groups from participating in the digital economy (Alexandrova-Kabadjova & Negrin, 2009).
Phase 3: Internet Banking and Digital Ledgers. The advent of the internet in the 1990s migrated banking from physical branches to web portals such as PayPal and online banking (De Battista, 2011). This era introduced the concept of the "Virtual Account" where value was stored in a database rather than a vault. Its Key Contribution was enabling remote transfers and e-commerce, allowing capital to move across borders without physical transport. Nevertheless, a significant Limitation persisted as early internet banking was designed primarily for B2C (Business-to-Consumer) or B2B contexts. P2P transfers remained cumbersome because they required long account numbers and suffered from delayed settlement times, making them unsuitable for real-time split-bill settlement (Khando et al., 2022).
Phase 4: Mobile-First Ecosystems and the "Super-App" Phenomenon. The current epoch is defined by the rise of mobile-first ecosystems like GrabPay, Touch 'n Go, and WeChat Pay. These platforms leverage the ubiquity of smartphones to bypass traditional banking rails and have evolved from simple stored-value cards into comprehensive "Super-Apps" (Chellappan et al., 2025). This era is characterized by Market Saturation where unlike single-purpose tools, Super-Apps aggressively practice Platform Envelopment Strategy by absorbing adjacent services such as investment, insurance, and retail payments into a single interface. However, this breadth has created a "Social Gap". Despite their vast feature sets, these platforms often lack depth in specific niche areas. Current trends prioritize B2C and simple P2P transactions which treat users as isolated nodes. They frequently neglect "Social FinTech" and complex, many-to-many-group financial management. This forces users to rely on disjointed external tools for collaborative expense tracking.
2.1.1.2 Technological Disruption Theory. To critically analyse this evolution, one must apply Clayton Christensen’s Theory of Disruptive Innovation. According to this theory, a disruptive innovation is not merely an "improvement" (sustaining innovation) but a mechanism that transforms a market by introducing simplicity, convenience, and affordability to underserved populations (Christensen, 1997).
1. Low-End Disruption (Cost & Infrastructure.  Traditional banking operates on a high-cost infrastructure model. Mobile wallets disrupted this by leveraging existing user hardware (smartphones). By removing the need for physical POS terminals, e-wallets lowered the transaction cost to near zero (Hopalı et al., 2022). This allowed them to capture the "low-end" market—micro-transactions (e.g., RM2 for street food)—which traditional credit cards ignored. For the proposed FYP, this low-cost structure is what makes frequent, small-value split payments economically viable.
2. New-Market Disruption (Financial Inclusion).  Christensen argues that disruption often targets "non-consumers." Mobile wallets created a "new market" by decoupling financial access from traditional credit history, allowing unbanked users to participate in the digital economy using only a mobile number (Beck, 2020). This aligns directly with SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), as it democratizes financial tools, allowing informal groups (students, families) to manage collective finances with the same sophistication as corporate entities (Choudhary et al., 2025).
3. Addressing the "Innovator's Dilemma". While Super-Apps have successfully disrupted the market, they now face the "Innovator's Dilemma” becoming too large to address niche user needs effectively (Christensen, 1997). They often rely on rigid, massive architectures that make adding "complex, stateful logic" (like hybrid split-bill engines) risky. This project addresses that specific gap by proposing a Microservice Architecture, specifically designed to handle the Collaborative Intelligence that monolithic giants struggle to implement (Cusumano, 2010).
2.1.1.3 The "Super-App" Phenomenon and Platform Envelopment. The contemporary digital landscape is characterized by the emergence and dominance of "Super-Apps," integrated mobile ecosystems that aggregate disparate services into a unified interface (Ye, 2023). Originating from the "East-to-West" tech diffusion model, exemplified by platforms like WeChat and Grab, this phenomenon represents a fundamental shift in software economics where Asian markets have led the transition while western markets play catch-up (Hasselwander, 2024). Applications that began as single-purpose utilities, such as transit payment cards or ride-hailing services, have aggressively expanded their functional perimeters to become comprehensive lifestyle operating systems. This evolution is not random but follows a strategic trajectory known as Platform Envelopment (Eisenmann et al., 2011).
Platform Envelopment Strategy involves a dominant platform provider entering adjacent markets by bundling new functionality with its existing core service, effectively absorbing standalone competitors (Eisenmann et al., 2011). Super-Apps utilize their captured user base and established payment infrastructure to "envelop" neighbouring sectors. For instance, a platform may leverage its high-frequency transport users to cross-sell low-frequency financial products like micro-insurance, investment funds, and content streaming (Lee et al., 2025). By capitalizing on economies of scope, these platforms reduce the marginal cost of user acquisition for new services, creating a self-reinforcing cycle where the app becomes the primary gateway for the user's entire digital life (Bank for International Settlements, 2022).
2.1.1.4 Gap Analysis: The Breadth-Over-Depth Paradox. While Platform Envelopment facilitates rapid market expansion, it introduces a critical structural weakness defined as the "Breadth-Over-Depth" paradox. In the pursuit of offering a comprehensive suite of services, Super-Apps often sacrifice functional sophistication for widespread utility. These ecosystems primarily optimize for Business-to-Consumer (B2C) interactions—such as merchant QR scanning—because these transactions generate consistent Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) revenue (Gao et al., 2024). Consequently, features related to Peer-to-Peer (P2P) interaction and collaborative finance are often treated as secondary utilities rather than core products (Sun, 2023).
This prioritization results in a functional disconnect for users requiring complex financial management. While major e-wallets enable users to transfer funds, their internal logic is often rudimentary, lacking the architectural agility to handle "stateful" group dynamics. They typically fail to support real-world collaborative scenarios, such as hybrid bill splitting (combining tax calculations with fixed-amount deductions) or multi-currency group settlements. This creates a "mile-wide, inch-deep" ecosystem: widely accessible but insufficiently specialized (Rietveld et al., 2019). This specific functional gap highlights the necessity for a dedicated, microservice-based solution capable of handling the algorithmic complexity of social finance that monolithic Super-Apps neglect (Megargel et al., 2020).
2.1.1.5 Gap Analysis: Structural Limitations of P2P and B2C Models. Beyond the functional breadth issues, a fundamental architectural deficiency exists in how current digital wallets model financial relationships. The dominant paradigm in existing FinTech systems is predicated on dyadic interactions, specifically Business-to-Consumer (B2C) and Peer-to-Peer (P2P) models. In a B2C transaction, the logic is linear and unidirectional: a consumer transfers value to a merchant in exchange for goods. Similarly, P2P transactions are strictly bilateral, involving a single sender and a single receiver with a direct settlement path.
However, Collaborative Group Finance operates on a Many-to-Many topology which is computationally distinct from isolated P2P transfers. A real-world social financial event, such as a group vacation, involves circular dependencies where multiple agents act simultaneously as creditors and debtors. Current platforms lack the native data structures to represent this complex state. Instead, they force users to decompose a singular multi-party event into a series of disconnected, manual P2P transfers. This architectural rigidity results in a loss of transactional context, as the system fails to recognize that ten individual transfers are part of one cohesive financial event.
2.1.1.6 Network Theory and Metcalfe’s Law. To quantify the structural limitations of current payment models, this research references Metcalfe’s Law, a core principle of network theory which states that the value of a telecommunications network is proportional to the square of the number of connected users (Metcalfe, 2013).
V∝n^2  
In the context of financial applications, the "value" is derived from the potential for transaction execution and liquidity flow between the connected nodes or users (Peterson, 2018).
Current e-wallet architectures significantly underutilize this potential by treating users as isolated nodes that only interact linearly—a structure often defined as a "pipeline" model (Van Alstyne et al., 2016). By restricting interactions to simple 1-to-1 Peer-to-Peer transfers, these platforms artificially cap the network value at a linear growth rate. In contrast, the proposed "Collection" module aggregates users into Social Clusters or fully connected sub-graphs. Within these clusters, the interaction density increases exponentially because every member can transact with every other member simultaneously through the split-logic engine. By architecting the system to recognize and facilitate these interconnected mesh clusters, the proposed solution leverages the full mathematical potential of Metcalfe’s Law, thereby maximizing user engagement and transaction velocity within the ecosystem (Zhang et al., 2015).
2.1.1.7 Financial Inclusion and Alignment with SDG 8. In contrast, mobile-first financial tools lower these barriers to entry by leveraging ubiquitous smartphone penetration to provide essential banking utility without the prerequisite of physical brick-and-mortar infrastructure (Tay et al., 2022).
This democratization of financial services aligns directly with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth). 2.1.1.8 Formalization of the Informal Economy. To further understand the socio-economic impact of this system, it is necessary to analyse the concept of the "Formalization of the Informal Economy." A significant portion of economic activity, particularly within social groups and micro-enterprises, occurs through informal mechanisms. These transactions often rely on verbal agreements or "handshake" contracts regarding shared costs, such as splitting travel expenses or household utilities (Williams & Lansky, 2013). While socially convenient, these informal arrangements suffer from a lack of auditability and enforceability, leading to frequent disputes and financial leakage (Webb et al., 2009).
Digital collaborative tools serve to bridge this gap by transitioning these interactions from the informal to the formal domain. The system functions as an impartial arbiter that transforms subjective verbal agreements into objective, immutable digital records (Uglai, 2016). This process of digitization creates a transparent audit trail for every shared expense. Consequently, this formalization reduces the social friction associated with debt collection and protects the economic interests of all participants. By validating and recording every contribution, the system ensures that financial participation within the group is equitable and traceable, thereby fostering a more stable micro-economic environment for its users (Demirgüç-Kunt et al., 2018).
2.1.1.9 Technical Constraints in Legacy FinTech Systems. Despite the market dominance of established financial technology providers such as PayPal or early iterations of Touch 'n Go, a paradox exists where these resource-rich organizations fail to innovate in specific functional areas like collaborative finance. This functional stagnation is rarely due to a lack of capital or vision but is fundamentally rooted in the underlying architectural limitations of their legacy systems. Most first-generation and second-generation FinTech platforms were constructed during an era where stability and linear transaction processing were the primary design directives (Megargel et al., 2020). Consequently, their core engines are optimized for simple, atomic transactions where a single payer transfers funds to a single payee, often relying on centralized, monolithic database structures that inhibit flexibility (Awosan, 2025).
Implementing complex, stateful logic—such as the proposed hybrid split-payment logic requires the system to maintain a persistent "context" across multiple users and varied timeframes. For a legacy system, introducing this variable logic into the core transaction pipeline presents significant operational risks. The codebase often suffers from high viscosity, meaning that even minor functional additions require disproportionate effort in testing and deployment (Tapia et al., 2020). As a result, incumbent providers often perceive niche features like advanced bill splitting as high-risk and low reward, leading to a functional paralysis where they cannot easily adapt to the nuances of social finance (Laigner et al., 2025).
2.1.1.10 Technical Debt in Monolithic Architectures. To critically justify the need for a new system, it is essential to analyse the concept of Technical Debt within Monolithic Architectures. Ward Cunningham metaphorically defined technical debt as the implied cost of additional rework caused by choosing an easy (limited) solution now instead of using a better approach that would take longer (Cunningham, 1992). This concept has been further refined to describe the accumulation of sub-optimal design decisions that progressively impede software evolvability (Avgeriou et al., 2016).
Established players often operate on massive, monolithic codebases where the user interface, business logic, and data access layers are tightly coupled into a single deployable unit. In such an environment, the dependencies between modules are entangled (Fowler, 2014). Attempting to inject a complex "Split-Bill Algorithm" directly into a monolithic "Payment Service" creates a high risk of unintended side effects, potentially destabilizing the entire platform. This accumulation of technical debt acts as a barrier to innovation, often freezing legacy systems into a state of "functional paralysis" where the risk of deployment outweighs the value of new features (Kruchten et al., 2012).
The proposed project circumvents this limitation by adopting a Microservice Architecture. By decoupling the "Split Logic" into an independent service that communicates via APIs, the system can execute complex, experimental algorithms without endangering the core financial ledger (Newman, 2021). This architectural decision directly addresses the rigidity of legacy systems, ensuring that the platform remains flexible and scalable (Dragoni et al., 2017). 
2.1.2 Collaborative Financial Management (Social Finance)
2.1.2.1 The Paradigm of Social Finance and Shared Consumption. In the context of this research, the term "Social Finance" is distinguished from its traditional macroeconomic definition, which typically refers to sustainable investing (ESG) or impact funds. Instead, within the domain of collaborative systems, Social Finance refers to the micro-economic management of shared resources, liquidity, and obligations within small, trusted networks. These networks include domestic units (households), temporary collectives (travel groups), and co-living arrangements (student dormitories) (Agarwal & Mannil, 2023). Unlike formal business finance, which is governed by contracts and legal enforcement, social finance is governed by interpersonal trust and informal agreements, making it highly susceptible to ambiguity and mismanagement (Xu et al., 2023).
The Shift to Shared Consumption Models. The necessity for such systems is driven by a broader societal shift from individual ownership to shared consumption. The rise of the sharing economy has normalized the pooling of resources, a trend exemplified by platforms such as Airbnb, GrabShare, and ClassPass (Barbu et al., 2018). In this model, financial events are no longer atomic transactions where a single user pays for a single item; instead, they become composite events where one individual pays for a resource consumed by multiple people (Sun, 2024). This transition necessitates a digital framework capable of accurately tracking both resource pooling to identify contributors and cost distribution to determine consumers. Traditional individualistic banking tools fail to address this complexity, often resulting in social and financial inefficiencies such as the "unscrupulous diner's dilemma" (Gneezy et al., 2004).
2.1.2.2 Social Exchange Theory (Emerson). To understand the underlying dynamics of these interactions, this project applies Social Exchange Theory, primarily developed by sociologist Richard Emerson (1976). This theory posits that social behaviour is the result of an exchange process where individuals endeavour to maximize benefits and minimize costs (Emerson, 1976). In a healthy social relationship, these exchanges gravitate toward a state of equilibrium, where the distribution of rewards and costs is perceived as fair by all participants (Homans, 1958).
The Principle of Reciprocity. The core of this theory is "Reciprocity." When one member of a group covers a financial cost for another, a temporary asymmetry or "debt" is created. This obligation to repay is not merely economic but follows the universal "norm of reciprocity," which dictates that people should help those who have helped them (Gouldner, 1960).
Imbalance and Tension. If this financial imbalance is not rectified, the social exchange becomes "unbalanced," creating psychological tension and potential conflict. The debt ceases to be merely financial and becomes a social burden, leading to avoidance behaviours or the deterioration of the relationship (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005).
Systemic Application. The proposed E-Wallet system functions as an automated mediator designed to restore Social Equity. By providing an objective, immutable record of the debt and facilitating immediate settlement, the system accelerates the return to equilibrium. It removes the social friction of "remembering" and "asking," thereby preserving the social bond by ensuring that the principle of reciprocity is mathematically enforced (Cook & Rice, 2003).
2.1.2.3 Psychological Barriers in Shared Expense Recovery. While the mechanics of social finance are mathematical, the obstacles to efficient settlement are predominantly psychological. Lending money within social circles incurs "non-financial costs," primarily in the form of social capital depletion and emotional stress (McGraw & Tetlock, 2005). A significant barrier to debt recovery is the phenomenon known as the "Payer’s Burden." This occurs when the individual who covers the cost (the creditor) voluntarily absorbs the expense rather than requesting repayment. This behaviour stems from the fear of social judgment; the creditor avoids initiating the debt collection process to prevent being perceived as "petty," "stingy," or transactional, especially when the amounts are trivial but cumulative (Fiske, 1992).
Furthermore, friction often arises not from a debtor's unwillingness to pay, but from Cognitive Load and situational ambiguity. In informal settings, the terms of the split are rarely explicitly defined at the point of transaction (e.g., "Did we agree to split the tax equally, or is it proportional to the meal?"). This uncertainty creates a "Cost of Verification," where the mental effort required to recall, calculate, and verify the debt outweighs the value of the money itself (Sweller, 1988). Consequently, the debt is left unpaid due to inertia rather than malice, a concept closely linked to the "status quo bias" in behavioural economics (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988).
2.1.2.4 Ambiguity Aversion and Algorithmic Mediation. To address these barriers, the system design leverages the behavioural economics concept of Ambiguity Aversion, a theory originally formalized by Daniel Ellsberg. This theory posits that humans exhibit a strong preference for known risks over unknown risks (Ellsberg, 1961). In the context of shared expenses, ambiguity regarding how much is owed and when it should be paid creates psychological discomfort, leading to avoidance behaviour (Camerer & Weber, 1992).
Removal of Ambiguity. By digitizing the expense immediately at the point of sale, the system removes this ambiguity. The "debt" is instantly quantified and recorded, transforming a subjective social memory into an objective data point. This process aligns with the principle of reducing cognitive load to facilitate decision-making (Sweller, 2011).
The Neutral Third Party (Algorithmic Mediation). Crucially, the system acts as an emotionally neutral mediator. A text message from a friend asking for money is socially charged and can be interpreted as confrontational. In contrast, a standardized notification from a system is perceived as a procedural administrative prompt. By shifting the "locus of causality" from the friend to the algorithm, the system decouples the financial obligation from the personal relationship, significantly reducing the "Pain of Paying" and minimizing social friction (Prelec & Loewenstein, 1998; Zellermayer, 1996).
2.1.2.5 Taxonomy of Expense Division Strategies. To engineer a robust financial platform, it is necessary to move beyond colloquial understandings of "splitting bills" and establish a rigorous mathematical and functional classification of expense division. This taxonomy defines the "Algorithmic Rules" that the proposed system aims to implement. Current social finance scenarios generally adhere to one of three primary mathematical models (Moulin, 2004): 
A. The Egalitarian Model (Equal Split). The most rudimentary form of division is the Egalitarian Model, which dictates a uniform distribution of liability across all participating entities (Deutsch, 1975).
Mathematical Formulation
〖Cost〗_User=  Total/N
Where Total represents the aggregate bill amount and N represents the count of participants.
Operational Context
This model functions optimally in low-variance scenarios, such as splitting a taxi fare or a shared accommodation fee, where consumption is inherently identical.
Critique
While computationally simple, this model fails in scenarios exhibiting high Variance in Consumption. For example, in a dining context where one participant consumes a premium item (e.g., steak) and another consumes a low-cost item (e.g., salad), an equal split results in a "subsidy effect," creating perceived unfairness and financial disadvantage for the low consumer. This phenomenon is famously documented in behavioural economics as the "unscrupulous diner's dilemma" (Gneezy et al., 2004).
B. The Proportional Model (Ratio-Based Split). The Proportional Model distributes cost based on a weighted attribute, ensuring that liability is commensurate with a specific variable, such as individual income or specific consumption value.
Mathematical Formulation
〖Cost〗_User= Total ×(〖Weight〗_User/(∑_(i=1)^n▒〖Weight〗_i ))
Operational Context
This is essential for "Distributive Justice" or Equity Theory, which posits that outcomes should be proportional to inputs (Adams, 1965). It is commonly applied in household management (splitting rent based on salary ratios) or itemized dining (paying exactly for what was consumed + a ratio of the tax).
Critique
Despite its fairness, this model imposes a high Cognitive Load. Without digital assistance, manually calculating individual ratios and applying them to a sub-total (e.g., Service Charge) is prone to arithmetic errors, leading users to revert to the unfair but simple Egalitarian model (Feldman & Bartels, 2020).
C. The Hybrid/Adjustment Model (The Research Gap). The Hybrid Model represents the complex reality of social spending, where a single transaction contains both "Fixed" assignments (specific items) and "Shared" assignments (taxes, tips, shared appetizers).
Mathematical Formulation:
Total= ∑▒〖(〖Fixed〗_Assignments )+ ∑▒〖(Remainder/N_Subset )  〗〗
Gap Identification
This model highlights the critical functional gap in current E-Wallet architectures. Existing platforms typically force a binary choice: users must choose either "Split Equally" or "Split by Exact Amount" (Chellappan et al., 2025). They rarely support a Composite Logic where specific amounts are deducted first, and the remaining balance (e.g., a shared tax of 6%) is automatically distributed among a specific subset of users. The proposed system specifically targets this gap by engineering a split-logic engine capable of hierarchical processing.
2.1.2.6 Fair Division Theory (Steinhaus). To theoretically ground these algorithms, the project draws upon Fair Division Theory, a concept formalized by mathematician Hugo Steinhaus. This theory addresses the problem of dividing a set of resources among several people who have different entitlements (or preferences) such that each person receives their due share (Steinhaus, 1948).
Envy-Free Assignment. The goal of the system is to achieve an "Envy-Free" state, where no agent prefers the bundle of resources (or debts) assigned to another agent over their own (Brams & Taylor, 1996).
Transparency as Fairness. In a manual process, the "black box" of calculation breed’s suspicion. By digitizing the logic, the system automates Algorithmic Transparency. When a user can view the precise derivation of their debt (e.g., "Your share includes RM50 for Item A + RM3.00 (6% SST)"), they perceive the outcome as objective. The system validates the fairness of the Hybrid Model, ensuring that complex splits do not result in social friction due to perceived miscalculation (Shin, 2021).
 
2.1.3 Algorithmic Approaches to Expense Splitting (Mathematical Foundations)
The implementation of collaborative financial systems requires a rigorous approach to algorithmic design, moving beyond theoretical division to address the constraints of computational arithmetic. This section analyses the mathematical strategies employed to ensure precision and consistency.
2.1.3.1 Numerical Precision and the Allocation of Remainders. The primary challenge in digital finance is the "Penny Allocation Problem," which arises from the indivisibility of currency units when subjected to division operations. Unlike theoretical mathematics where division can produce infinite repeating decimals
10 ÷3=3.333…
Financial transactions are constrained by the smallest currency unit, typically two decimal places. When a finite sum is divided by a non-factorable denominator, a rounding discrepancy inevitably occurs due to the limitations of binary floating-point arithmetic used in standard computing environments (Goldberg, 1991).
This challenge is governed by the Conservation of Value, a fundamental accounting principle dictating that the sum of the distributed parts must exactly equal the original total. In the standard example of splitting RM10.00 among three participants, a standard rounding function would allocate RM3.33 to each user. However, summing these parts yields RM9.99, resulting in the "disappearance" of RM0.01. In a high-volume transactional system, these accumulated variances violate the zero-sum integrity of the financial ledger.
Total - ∑_(i=1)^n▒(〖Share〗_i ) =Remainder
10.00-(3.33+3.33+3.33)=0.01
To resolve this, the system employs a specific allocation algorithm, often referred to as the "Money Pattern," which distributes the remainder iteratively to ensure that no value is lost or created during the split (Fowler, 2002).
2.1.3.2 IEEE 754 vs. Arbitrary-Precision Arithmetic. To resolve the precision issues identified in the previous section, the system must address the inherent limitations of the IEEE 754 Floating-Point Standard, which governs how most modern processors handle real numbers.
Standard primitive data types, such as float or double, utilize binary fractions to approximate decimal values. While efficient for scientific calculations, this approximation often leads to critical precision errors in financial contexts.
For example,
The simple addition of 
0.1+0.2
may result in
0.30000000000000004
rather than expected
0.3
(Goldberg, 1991). 
Relying on these types for currency storage introduces a phenomenon known as "drift," where microscopic errors accumulate over large datasets. This renders them unsuitable for monetary applications where absolute exactness is required (Higham, 2002). Consequently, the proposed system explicitly rejects standard floating-point types in favour of Arbitrary-Precision Arithmetic, specifically utilizing Java’s BigDecimal class. Unlike binary floating-point representations, this approach ensures that numbers are stored as exact decimals, guaranteeing precision during complex multiplication or division operations (Bloch, 2018).
2.1.3.3 The Distributive Remainder Algorithm. Building upon the decision to use Arbitrary-Precision Arithmetic, the system implements the Distributive Remainder Algorithm to solve the allocation problem. This algorithm performs a three-step operation to ensure the ledger remains perfectly balanced without creating or destroying value.
1. Floor Calculation
Calculate the share rounded down to the nearest cent
(10 ÷3)=3.33
2. Residual Identification
Calculate the remainder by subtracting the sum of floor shares from the total
10.00-(3.33 ×3)=0.01
3. Iterative Allocation
The residual amount
0.01
is distributed sequentially to the participants until the remainder is exhausted
3.34+3.33+3.33=10.00
2.1.3.4 Hierarchical Arithmetic: The Order of Operations. In hybrid billing scenarios, where a single transaction encompasses both individual liabilities (e.g., specific meals) and collective obligations (e.g., shared appetizers, taxes), the precision of the final output is entirely dependent on the Order of Operations. Much like the standard algebraic precedence rules (PEMDAS), the system must adhere to a strict, non-commutative execution sequence. It is insufficient to merely "split" the total; the algorithm must sequentially isolate variables to prevent the misallocation of costs, a principle rooted in the accounting distinction between direct tracing and indirect allocation (Horngren et al., 2023).
The Algorithmic Logic Flow. To ensure mathematical integrity, the proposed split logic follows a rigid three-stage pipeline
1. Isolation of Fixed Variables
The algorithm first identifies and deducts all "Fixed Amounts" (specific items assigned to specific users) from the global total. This isolates the true "Common Base"—the remaining pool of funds that represents shared consumption. This step mirrors the "Separable Costs-Remaining Benefits" method used in cooperative game theory to ensure agents are only liable for costs they directly incur (Young, 1994).
2. Derivation of the Common Base Share
The remaining balance is then divided according to the selected rule (Equal or Proportional) among the relevant subsets of users.
3. Individualized Overhead Application
Crucially, percentage-based overheads (Tax, Service Charge) are applied individually to each user's composite share (Fixed + Base Share) rather than to the global total. This distinction is vital for fairness.
Computational Validation (The "Steak vs. Salad" Scenario). To demonstrate the necessity of this hierarchy, consider a scenario involving two users, A and B,

	User B: Orders a Salad (RM20.00)
	Total Bill: RM70.00 + RM7.00 (Tax) = RM77.00

If the tax (RM7.00) is split equally (Egalitarian Model), the calculation creates a disparity
〖Cost〗_UserB=20.00+  7.00/2=23.50

3.50/20.00=17.5%
Result: User B effectively subsidizes User A, as they are paying a higher effective tax rate than the mandated 10%. This exacerbates the "unscrupulous diner's dilemma" (Gneezy et al., 2004).

The proposed system applies the overhead after isolation, at the individual node level (Moulin, 2003)
〖Cost〗_UserB=20.00+(20.00 ×0.10)=22.00

As a result, fairness is preserved. By strictly adhering to this calculation hierarchy, the system ensures that each user pays tax strictly proportional to their own consumption, eliminating the mathematical subsidy effect.
2.1.3.5 Compound vs. Simple Distribution. A critical optimization within this hierarchy is the capability to distinguish between Simple and Compound distribution models. Financial regulations often dictate specific tax structures that require hierarchical calculation.
Tax-on-Tax. In many jurisdictions, including certain contexts in Malaysia, taxes are cumulative. For instance, the Sales and Service Tax (SST) may need to be calculated after the application of a 10% Service Charge.

By implementing this compound capability, the system ensures compliance with real-world receipt structures, preventing the "rounding drift" that occurs when simplified flat-rate models are applied to complex, multi-tiered taxation systems.
2.1.3.6 Optimization Concept: Transaction Netting and Settlement Efficiency. In the domain of collaborative finance, the volume of discrete financial events often outpaces the users' ability to settle them efficiently. In a travel group of five individuals, dozens of small transactions (e.g., meals, tickets, transport) may occur over a single weekend. If every debt were settled individually (Gross Settlement), the group would face significant "Settlement Friction," characterized by excessive banking fees, time latency, and the cognitive fatigue of managing multiple small transfers. To mitigate this, the system employs an optimization logic known as Bilateral Netting, a fundamental principle adapted from interbank clearing systems to minimize the volume of required payments (Bank for International Settlements, 1989).
The Netting Logic. Unlike "Gross Settlement," where every obligation is paid individually, Netting consolidates multiple reciprocal obligations between two parties into a single value.
For example,
	User A owes User B RM50.00 for dinner
	User B owes User A RM20.00 for a taxi ride
In a naive system, two separate transactions would occur. The proposed algorithm, however, calculates the Net Open Position:
〖Net〗_(A → B)= ∑▒〖(〖Debts〗_(A → B) )- 〗 ∑▒(〖Debts〗_(B → A) ) 
〖Net〗_(A → B)= 50.00-20.00=30.00
The system instructs User A to transfer exactly RM30.00 to User B. This effectively reduces the transaction count by 50% and reduces the total liquidity required to settle the debt.
From a theoretical perspective, this approach minimizes Settlement Risk and operational costs. By aggregating debts into a single "Settlement Batch," the system ensures that users are not overwhelmed by micro-transactions. While it avoids the high computational complexity of multilateral graph simplification (such as removing circular dependencies across three or more users), Bilateral Netting offers a robust, low-complexity solution that significantly improves the user experience by ensuring that only the mathematical difference is transferred (Chakravorti, 2010; McAndrews & Roberds, 1995).
 
2.1.4 Domain Research: Multi-Currency Systems and Exchange Mechanics
2.1.4.1 Fundamentals of Digital Currency Exchange. The integration of multi-currency support into an e-wallet system necessitates adherence to global standardization protocols to ensure interoperability and data consistency. The foundational framework for this is the ISO 4217 Standard, established by the International Organization for Standardization. This standard defines the three-letter alphabetic codes (e.g., MYR, USD, SGD) and the specific minor unit precision (decimal places) for every recognized currency (International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 2015).
In a monolithic banking system, currency logic is often hardcoded. However, in a distributed microservice environment, the system must treat currency not merely as a label, but as a dynamic object with variable attributes. The primary challenge in this domain is the digitization of the Foreign Exchange (FX) Market, a decentralized global marketplace that determines the relative values of different currencies. Unlike the stock market, the FX market operates 24/7, meaning the "value" of a wallet balance held in a foreign denomination is in a state of perpetual flux (Levich, 2001). For the proposed system, which supports MYR, SGD, and USD, understanding these dynamics is critical to preventing "Value Erosion" during cross-border settlements.
2.1.4.2 Temporal Volatility and Transaction Exposure. A critical risk factor in multi-currency collaborative finance is Transaction Exposure, defined as the risk that exchange rates will fluctuate between the time a financial obligation is incurred (e.g., creating a bill) and the time it is settled (e.g., paying the debt) (Madura, 2020).
The "Time-Lag" Scenario. Consider a scenario where a "Trip Collection" is denominated in USD, but a participant holds only MYR
	Time T0 (Bill Creation): 1 USD = 4.50 MYR. The user owes $10.00 (RM45.00).
	Time T1 (Settlement 1 week later): 1 USD = 4.70 MYR. The user now effectively owes RM47.00.
This volatility introduces a "Fairness Paradox." If the system fixes the rate at T0, the payee (receiver) loses value if the currency strengthens. If the system uses the rate at T1, the payer pays more than anticipated. To resolve this, the proposed system adopts a Real-Time Settlement Model. The conversion rate is locked strictly now of payment execution (T1), shifting the currency risk to the user holding the foreign asset, which aligns with standard international accounting practices (Shapiro, 2013).
2.1.4.3 Algorithmic Triangulation (Cross-Currency Logic). In an ideal scenario, direct exchange rates exist for every currency pair (e.g., SGD/MYR). However, in a system scaling to multiple currencies, maintaining direct rates for every permutation
(N(N-1)pairs)
becomes computationally inefficient and data intensive. To solve this, modern financial systems utilize Triangular Arbitrage Logic or "Cross-Rates" via a vehicle currency, typically the US Dollar (USD) (Bekaert & Hodrick, 2017).
The Mathematical Model. If a user wishes to pay an SGD obligation using MYR, and the system only streams rates against USD, the algorithm derives the cross-rate dynamically:
〖Rate〗_((MYR/SGD))=  〖Rate〗_((MYR/USD))/〖Rate〗_((SGD/USD)) 
This derivation is computationally more efficient for the microservice architecture, as the system only needs to fetch and cache rates against a single base currency (USD) to calculate conversion for any other supported pair. This reduces external API calls and latency, adhering to the efficiency requirements of a distributed system.
2.1.4.4 The "Oracle Problem" and Data Freshness. From a software engineering perspective, a closed-loop system (like an internal database) cannot inherently know the external market value of a currency. This is known as the Oracle Problem. The system requires an authoritative external source of truth (e.g., Open Exchange Rates API, Bloomberg) to inject market data into the internal ledger (Caldarelli, 2020).
Caching vs. Accuracy Trade-off. Relying on live external API calls for every transaction introduces two risks:
	Latency: External HTTP requests are slow (200-500ms), degrading the user experience.
	Rate Limiting: Commercial APIs impose cost limits on request frequency.
To mitigate this, the system implementation must utilize a Time-to-Live (TTL) Caching Strategy (e.g., using Redis). Exchange rates are fetched and stored for a fixed window (e.g., 60 minutes). During this window, all internal conversions use the cached rate. This introduces a deliberate "Soft Real-Time" consistency, accepting a microscopic deviation in exchange accuracy in exchange for massive gains in system performance and reliability (Tanenbaum & Van Steen, 2017).
2.1.4.5 Spread Management and the "Mid-Market" Illusion. Academic research often utilizes the "Mid-Market Rate", the midpoint between the Buy (Bid) and Sell (Ask) prices—as the true value of currency. However, commercial systems (PayPal, Wise, Banks) never transact at the mid-market rate; they apply a Spread or margin to cover volatility risk and generate revenue (Krugman et al., 2012).
〖Rate〗_Final= 〖Rate〗_Mid  ×(1+Margin%)
In the proposed system, while the focus is on "Collaborative Finance" rather than profit generation, acknowledging the spread is vital for simulation realism. If the system were to use raw mid-market rates, it would simulate a "Zero-Fee" environment that does not exist in the real economy. Therefore, the architectural design allows for a configurable "System Spread" parameter to be applied to the raw data fetched from the Oracle, demonstrating the system's capability to model real-world fintech business logic.
 
2.2 Similar System
To establish a benchmark for the proposed "Secure Distributed Microservice-Based E-Wallet," this research conducts a comparative analysis of three dominant market incumbents: Splitwise, Touch 'n Go eWallet, and Revolut. These platforms were selected based on their alignment with the three core functional pillars of this project: Collaborative Expense Management, Domestic Digital Payments, and Multi-Currency Architecture.
2.2.1  Splitwise (The Ledger-Based Model)
Splitwise serves as the primary case study for the "Ledger-Based" architectural model in collaborative finance. Widely regarded as the industry standard for informal debt tracking, it represents a specialized class of software designed solely for the management of social financial obligations. Unlike full-stack payment processors, Splitwise operates on a decoupling principle, separating the informational layer of debt recording from the transactional layer of monetary settlement.
2.2.1.1 Characteristics of the System. The operational logic of Splitwise is defined by three distinct architectural characteristics that differentiate it from standard electronic wallet ecosystems. These characteristics represent a specific design philosophy that prioritizes informational flexibility over transactional rigidity.
A. Decoupled Settlement Architecture. The fundamental architectural distinction of Splitwise is its adherence to a Non-Custodial Data Model. Unlike traditional e-wallets or banking applications which tightly couple the "Ledger of Record" with the actual "Store of Value," Splitwise separates these concerns entirely.
The system functions exclusively as an Information Broker, maintaining a "Shadow Ledger" or virtualized representation of financial obligations without possessing the legal or technical infrastructure to hold, process, or transfer user funds (Tapscott & Tapscott, 2016). This architecture creates a deliberate operational disconnect known as the "Settlement Gap." While the application can mathematically calculate a zero-sum state where all debts are resolved, it lacks the execution capabilities to enforce this state. Consequently, the actual transfer of liquidity must occur via external, unrelated payment rails such as physical cash, interbank transfers, or third-party gateways like PayPal. From a system design perspective, this reduces the platform's regulatory burden by avoiding banking license requirements, but it significantly increases the transactional friction for the user, who is forced to manually synchronize their external payments with internal ledger updates (Arner et al., 2015).
B. Graph-Based Debt Simplification (Multilateral Netting). Perhaps the most technically significant feature of the platform is its proprietary Debt Simplification Algorithm. 
In computer science terms, the system treats the user group as a Directed Weighted Graph, where users represent vertices

In a standard gross settlement model, if User A owes User B and User B owes User C, two distinct transactions are required. The Splitwise algorithm treats this as a flow network problem. By analysing the topology of the graph, it identifies redundancy loops and performs a Transitive Reduction to "short circuit" the intermediate nodes. It calculates the Net Open Position of every vertex and restructures the edges to minimize the total number of transactions required to clear the graph.
For instance, the algorithm will instruct User A to pay User C directly, bypassing User B entirely. This process optimizes the group's "Liquidity Efficiency" by reducing the total volume of capital that must move between parties and minimizing the external transaction costs associated with settling the group's obligations (Bank for International Settlements, 2008).
C. Asynchronous State Management (Eventual Consistency). To accommodate the specific behaviours of travellers and social groups, the system employs an Asynchronous State Model that prioritizes system availability over immediate consistency, a design choice often analysed through the lens of the CAP Theorem (Brewer, 2012). Unlike banking systems which strictly require ACID (Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability) transactions and real-time connectivity to prevent errors like double-spending, Splitwise operates on a model of Eventual Consistency. 
This "Offline-First" design allows users to record expenses in a local state on their device without an active internet connection. Upon re-establishing connectivity, the system initiates a synchronization process to merge the local state with the cloud ledger (Vogels, 2009). While this architecture significantly enhances the User Experience (UX) by removing the latency of real-time verification—making it highly resilient in low-connectivity environments such as international travel—it introduces the unavoidable risk of "State Conflict." In scenarios where two users edit the same debt simultaneously, the system relies on conflict resolution logic or manual user intervention to reconcile the divergent ledger states.

2.2.2 Touch 'n Go eWallet (The Super-App Model)
Touch 'n Go (TnG) eWallet serves as the definitive case study for the "Super-App" architectural model within the Malaysian FinTech landscape (Lai, 2022). 2.2.2.1 Characteristics of the System. The operational framework of Touch 'n Go eWallet is defined by three core characteristics that prioritize liquidity and security over social flexibility.
A. Licensed Stored-Value Facility (E-Money Issuer)Fundamentally, the system operates as a Licensed E-Money Issuer under the regulatory framework of Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM). Technically defined as a "Stored-Value Facility," the system operates on a pre-funded model where users must exchange fiat currency for digital value held in a pooled custodial account prior to transacting (Bank Negara Malaysia [BNM], 2025). Unlike credit-based facilities where debt is incurred and settled later, this asset-backed architecture ensures zero settlement risk for merchants but imposes a strict "Liquidity Constraint" on the user
 A transaction, including a collaborative bill split, cannot be initiated unless the user possesses sufficient liquid funds at that exact moment. While this model secures the ecosystem, it limits financial flexibility, as the system cannot record a debt for future repayment; it can only process an immediate transfer of existing value (Oseni & Ali, 2019).
B. QR-Based Interoperability (The DuitNow Standard). A critical technical characteristic is the system's integration with the DuitNow National QR Standard, which leverages the "Network Effect" to achieve high market ubiquity (Katz & Shapiro, 1985). By adhering to this interoperable protocol, the platform functions across a unified merchant acquirer network, effectively resolving the "acceptance fragmentation" issue that plagued early-generation e-wallets (Payments Network Malaysia [PayNet], 2024). 
Architecturally, the system utilizes a "Push" Payment Model, where the payer must actively initiate and authorize the transfer of funds. This contrasts with "Pull" mechanisms (like direct debits or credit card holds) and significantly impacts the design of automated splitting features, as every distinct financial event requires active user authorization, preventing the passive, automated settlement of debts often required in social finance (Bank for International Settlements, 2012).
C. Synchronous "Atomic" Splitting. The platform offers collaborative features such as "Money Packet" or a basic "Split Bill" function; however, these are strictly Transactional rather than Stateful. In computer science terms, the splitting function is designed as an Atomic Operation, meaning the transaction executes completely and immediately, or it fails entirely (Silberschatz et al., 2020). When a split request is acknowledged, the system attempts to deduct funds instantly from the participant's balance.
Crucially, the system does not support the creation of an "IOU" (I Owe You) record or a "Pending" debt state. Consequently, if a participant's wallet balance is insufficient, the social transaction fails. This lack of an intermediate debt state renders the system unsuitable for extended social events (e.g., a week-long trip) where settlement is typically deferred until the conclusion of the event, forcing users to rely on external ledgers to track the accumulation of costs (Lampson, 1981).

2.2.4 Conclusion and Research Gap
The comparative analysis of Splitwise, Touch 'n Go eWallet, and Revolut reveals a distinct functional fragmentation within the current collaborative finance landscape (Gomber et al., 2017). While each system excels in a specific domain including Logic, Liquidity, or Currency, none offer unified architecture capable of addressing the holistic needs of the modern "Social Traveler" (Arner et al., 2015).
This fragmentation can be modelled as a "FinTech Trilemma," where current market solutions are architecturally forced to compromise on one of three critical pillars:
The Logic Gap (represented by Touch 'n Go). Payment-centric "Super-Apps" possess the license and infrastructure to move money instantly (Atomic Settlement) but lack the data structures to handle complex social contracts. Their monolithic architecture restricts them to binary "Equal Split" logic, rendering them mathematically unfair for heterogeneous consumption, such as separating alcohol costs or tiered taxation (Richardson, 2018; Gneezy et al., 2004)
The Settlement Gap (represented by Splitwise). Ledger-centric applications offer the necessary Algorithmic Sophistication to model complex debts (e.g., Percentage Splits, Adjustments) but lack the custodial authority to execute the transfer (Swart, 2019). This decoupling creates a "Split-Brain" state where the digital record often desynchronizes from financial reality due to user errors (Silberschatz et al., 2020).
The Accessibility Gap (represented by Revolut). Neobanks provide the necessary Cross-Border Agility to mitigate exchange rate losses but impose high regulatory barriers (e-KYC) and operate as closed loops (Temelkov, 2020). They lack the "Collection-Based" flexibility to manage extended events, forcing users to treat a week-long trip as a series of disconnected, individual transactions (Bank for International Settlements, 2020).
Research Gap Justification. Consequently, a clear research gap exists for a hybrid system that bridges these silos. There is currently no accessible platform that integrates the complex splitting logic of a ledger, the immediate settlement of an e-wallet, and the multi-currency intelligence of a neobank into a single workflow. The proposed "Secure Distributed Microservice-Based E-Wallet" addresses this specific void. By adopting a Microservice Architecture, the proposed system can decouple the "Split Logic" from the "Payment Core," allowing it to support the high-level mathematical fairness of Splitwise without sacrificing the transactional integrity of Touch 'n Go, all while operating within a multi-currency context (Newman, 2021; Dragoni et al., 2017).

CHAPTER 3: METHODOLOGY
3.1 System Development Methodology
The selection of an appropriate system development methodology is a critical determinant in the successful execution of software engineering projects, particularly those involving complex, distributed architectures. A methodology serves not merely as a timeline, but as a rigid framework that governs the entire lifecycle of the application, which is from the initial elicitation of requirements to the final deployment and maintenance (Sommerville, 2016). It defines the protocols for process management, quality assurance, and the systematic transformation of theoretical objectives into functional deliverables (Pressman & Maxim, 2020). For this project, the methodology must accommodate the inherent complexity of a microservice-based environment, where multiple independent components must be developed, tested, and integrated continuously without compromising the integrity of the financial ledger.
3.1.1 Methodology Choice and Justification
To address the dynamic nature of the "Secure Distributed Microservice-Based E-Wallet System," this project adopts the Agile Scrum Methodology. Unlike traditional linear models such as Waterfall, which require a complete and frozen set of requirements before development begins, Agile Scrum operates on an iterative and incremental basis (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). This approach partitions the development lifecycle into discrete, time-boxed iterations known as "Sprints," typically lasting two to four weeks. Each sprint results in a potentially shippable product increment, allowing for continuous assessment and refinement of the system’s functionality (Rubin, 2012).
The justification for selecting Agile Scrum over the Waterfall model is threefold. First, the project involves high technical uncertainty, specifically regarding the implementation of the "Hybrid Split-Payment Logic" and "Cross-Currency Settlement." These features require experimental algorithmic design where the optimal solution often emerges through trial and error. A linear Waterfall approach would be catastrophic here, as a flaw discovered in the testing phase would require a costly regression to the design phase (Balaji & Murugaiyan, 2012). Scrum, conversely, allows for rapid prototyping and immediate rectification of algorithmic errors within the same sprint cycle (Highsmith, 2002).
Second, the microservice architecture itself demands an iterative approach. Developing the entire backend (Service Registry, API Gateway, Auth Service, Core Banking Service) simultaneously is impractical for a single developer. Scrum enables the decomposition of the system into manageable modules, allowing specific services to be deployed independently (Newman, 2021). For instance, the "User Authentication Service" can be fully developed, tested, and finalized in Sprint 1 before the developer proceeds to the "Collection Manager Service" in Sprint 2. This modular progression ensures that the system grows in stability and complexity organically, reducing the risk of "integration hell" at the end of the project lifecycle (Fowler, 2014).
Finally, the academic nature of a Final Year Project (FYP) imposes strict time constraints and regular supervisor reviews. Agile Scrum aligns perfectly with this academic cadence. The "Sprint Review" mechanism serves as a formal checkpoint where the developer can demonstrate progress to the supervisor, receive feedback, and adjust the "Product Backlog" accordingly (Sutherland, 2014). This feedback loop ensures that the project remains aligned with the research objectives and mitigates the risk of delivering a product that fails to meet the initial scope..
3.1.2 Phases of System Development
The development process is structured into five distinct phases, adapted from the standard Scrum lifecycle to fit the context of an individual academic project (Rubin, 2012).
3.1.2.1 Project Planning and Requirement Elicitation (The Product Backlog. The initial phase focuses on the comprehensive identification of system objectives and the creation of the Product Backlog. This involves translating the high-level research goals—such as "multi-currency support" and "hierarchical bill splitting"—into granular "User Stories." Each user story describes a specific feature from the end-user's perspective, accompanied by defined Acceptance Criteria (Cohn, 2004). For example, a user story for the wallet module might be defined as: "As a user, I want to view my balance in SGD so that I can track my foreign assets."
During this phase, the developer also conducts a feasibility study to define the technical constraints. This includes selecting the technology stack (Spring Boot, React, MySQL) and establishing the "Definition of Done" (DoD). For this project, the DoD mandates that every implemented feature must pass JUnit tests and maintain ACID compliance before it is considered complete (Rubin, 2012). The outcome of this phase is a prioritized Product Backlog, an ordered list of all desired features, ranked by their necessity to the core function of the e-wallet.
3.1.2.2 Architectural Design and Sprint Planning. Before coding commences, a robust architectural foundation is established to support the distributed nature of the system. This phase involves the creation of structural diagrams, including the Entity Relationship Diagram (ERD) to model the complex relationships between Users, Wallets, Collections, and Bills. Crucially, the Microservice Architecture is mapped out, defining the communication protocols between independent services—for example, specifying how the "Bill Service" requests user validation from the "Auth Service" via REST API (Richardson, 2018).
Simultaneously, the first Sprint Planning session is executed. The developer selects a subset of high-priority items from the Product Backlog to form the "Sprint Backlog." For the initial sprint, the focus is typically on infrastructural setup—establishing the Service Registry (Eureka), the API Gateway, and the Config Server. This ensures that the scaffolding for the distributed system is operational before business logic is injected (Newman, 2021). This phase transforms abstract requirements into concrete technical blueprints, reducing ambiguity during implementation.
3.1.2.3 Iterative Implementation (The Sprints) . This phase represents the core construction of the software, executed through a series of cyclical sprints.
Sprint Cycle 1 (Core Infrastructure). The focus is on setting up the Spring Boot microservice ecosystem. The developer implements the "Discovery Service" to allow dynamic service registration and the "API Gateway" to route external requests (Richardson, 2018).
Sprint Cycle 2 (User & Wallet Management). The "User Service" is developed to handle registration and JWT (JSON Web Token) authentication. Concurrently, the "Wallet Service" is built to manage multi-currency balances, implementing the BigDecimal logic to ensure mathematical precision and prevent floating-point errors (Bloch, 2018).
Sprint Cycle 3 (Collection & Split Logic). This is the most technically intensive cycle. The "Collection Service" is implemented to handle group dynamics. The complex "Distributive Remainder Algorithm" is coded to handle the logic of splitting bills, ensuring that tax calculations and remainders are allocated correctly according to the defined mathematical models.
Sprint Cycle 4 (Frontend Integration). The React mobile-web interface is developed. The frontend components are connected to the backend microservices via RESTful endpoints, creating the visual interface for the "Collection" and "Statement" modules.
3.1.2.4 Testing and Quality Assurance . In an Agile framework, testing is not a final phase but a continuous activity embedded within every sprint (Crispin & Gregory, 2009). However, a dedicated phase is allocated for comprehensive System Integration Testing (SIT). Because the system is distributed, unit tests (using JUnit and Mockito) are insufficient on their own. The developer must verify that the independent microservices communicate correctly. This involves "Contract Testing" to ensure that the JSON data format sent by the "Bill Service" matches exactly what the "Wallet Service" expects to receive (Newman, 2021).
Furthermore, specific stress tests are conducted on the "Split-Payment Engine." The developer simulates edge-case scenarios—such as a bill split among 3 users with a 10% tax and a 6% service charge—to verify that the "Money Pattern" algorithm correctly handles the remainder without losing a single cent. User Acceptance Testing (UAT) is also simulated, where the developer walks through the entire user journey, from creating an account to settling a cross-currency debt, ensuring the flow is intuitive and error-free.
3.1.2.5 Deployment and Revie. The final phase involves the deployment of the application components into a containerized environment using Docker. Each microservice is packaged into a Docker image and orchestrated to run simultaneously, simulating a live production environment (Merkel, 2014).
Once the system is operational, a final "Sprint Review" or Retrospective is conducted. The developer evaluates the final deliverable against the initial Product Backlog to ensure all core requirements have been met. Any known limitations or technical debt incurred during development are documented for future work. This phase concludes with the generation of the final documentation and the readiness of the system for academic presentation.
3.2 Data Gathering Design
Data gathering is a pivotal phase in the methodology, providing the empirical foundation required to validate problem statements and define user requirements. For this project, a quantitative approach was selected to efficiently aggregate user feedback regarding current limitations in e-wallet functionality and validate the proposed features of the Secure Distributed Microservice-Based E-Wallet System (Creswell & Creswell, 2018).
3.2.1 Data Gathering Techniques
The primary instrument selected for this research is a Quantitative Questionnaire. This technique was chosen for its ability to reach a geographically dispersed audience and generate statistically significant data regarding user behaviours, pain points, and feature preferences (Saunders et al., 2019). Unlike qualitative interviews, which explore depth, the questionnaire allows for the objective measurement of the prevalence of specific issues—such as the difficulty of calculating Service Tax (SST) manually or the frequency of cross-currency friction—across a broad demographic (Queirós et al., 2017)
3.2.2 Participants and Procedure
The study involved a minimum of 30 participants, primarily comprising university students and young professionals. This sample size adheres to the "rule of thumb" for small-scale behavioural research, which suggests that a sample size larger than 30 is sufficient to assume a normal distribution for statistical analysis (Roscoe, 1975). This specific demographic was targeted as they represent the most active user base for collaborative financial tools, frequently engaging in shared group activities (e.g., dining, travel, house-sharing) and possessing the technological literacy to utilize advanced digital financial instruments.
Respondents were recruited using purposive and convenience sampling techniques. This non-probability sampling method ensured that the data was drawn specifically from relevant users who possess actual experience with e-wallets, rather than a random population that might lack the necessary context (Etikan et al., 2016). Invitations containing the survey link were disseminated through academic networks and digital communication platforms (e.g., WhatsApp, Telegram). Participation was strictly voluntary, with informed consent obtained prior to the commencement of the survey to adhere to ethical research standards (Resnik, 2015)
.Data Collection Procedure Data was collected using a structured online questionnaire hosted on Google Forms. The instrument consisted of 25 items categorized into five distinct sections:
	Demographics: To establish user profiles (Age, Occupation, Wallet Ownership).
	Current Usage & Limitations: To assess the frequency of use and satisfaction with existing market solutions.
	Complex Split-Payment Requirements: To quantify the difficulty users face with tax calculations and hybrid division rules.
	Multi-Currency Challenges: To identify specific friction points in cross-border transactions.
	Proposed System Acceptance: To validate user interest in specific deliverables like "Collections" and "Direct Deductions."
The survey utilized a mix of 5-point Likert scales, multiple-choice questions, and checklists to quantitatively assess user pain points and feature preferences regarding collaborative e-wallet management (Boone & Boone, 2012).

To ensure the reliability and validity of the data, the questionnaire design was verified by the project supervisor (content validity).
